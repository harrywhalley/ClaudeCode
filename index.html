Literature & Contextual Reviews

Literature reviews are about engaging with The Field - like a conversation with papers and books. You should agree, disagree, extend, refute and compare the research.

Literature Review vs. Contextual Review

Literature Review

A selective, integrated analysis and synthesis of what has been researched and published on a particular topic. Like surveying a landscape from a helicopter.

Contextual Review

Builds a contextual framework using selected literature as "bricks and mortar" to create a foundation for your study. Includes your own practice, process, and material conditions of making.

Three Ways to Structure a Literature Review

📖

Narrative

Start broad, narrow down like a funnel. Begin with the 'forest of ideas', follow paths and trails, arrive at the clearing where your research questions stand.

Best for: Traditional theses with clear progression
📅

Chronological

Use history as structure. Start with foundational studies, show progression through time, highlight current gaps and new problems that lead to your questions.

Best for: Historical or developmental research
🎯

Thematic

Organize around themes, topics, or issues. Like a Venn diagram - find the overlaps where themes converge or conflict to locate your research questions.

Best for: Interdisciplinary or complex topics
⚠️

Top Tip #1

A literature review that describes rather than reviews is a common cause of major corrections. Be critical, not just descriptive!

From Notes to Literature Review

Good notes are analytical. They highlight tensions, ask questions, group ideas by relevance, and trace how concepts evolve across texts.

Topic-Concept Grid

Map sources against key conceptual categories to see patterns, gaps, and shifts across texts

Source-to-Theory Mapping

Connect specific elements you're analyzing to theoretical lenses and theorists

Research Questions

From Literature Review → Research Gap → Research Question

💡

Top Tip #3

Don't overdo it on research questions. You should have a keyword that encapsulates each question for you.

A Good Research Question is:

✓ Significant (not trivial)
✓ Answerable (but not answered already)
✓ Open-ended (but not open-ended forever)
✓ Contextualised (but not too localised)
✓ Conceptually anchored (but not abstract to the point of vagueness)

Methodology

Methodology is not your toolkit — it's the blueprint for why you picked those tools in the first place

If methods are the 'how', then methodology is the 'why behind the how'. It shapes how you see knowledge, truth, and evidence. Your methodology must be firm enough to anchor your study, yet flexible enough to evolve with it.

Practice as Research (PaR)

Your creative practice isn't separate from academic research — it can BE the research

Key Concepts in Practice as Research

1

Multi-Mode Knowing

Know-how: embodied, tacit knowledge gained through doing

Know-what: insights from critical reflection on practice

Know-that: conceptual knowledge from theory

2

Context & Lineage

To claim your work produces "new" knowledge, you must:

  • Locate your practice within a lineage
  • Conduct practice reviews (not just literature reviews)
  • Understand how your inquiry differs from existing work
3

Documentation is Evidence

Your process documentation isn't an afterthought — it's essential evidence:

  • Capture moments of breakthrough
  • Record iterative experiments
  • Note reflections throughout
  • Use multiple formats (sketches, video, objects, writing)

Is PaR Right for Your Project?

✅ Proceed with PaR if:

  • You have a clear research inquiry (not just creative production)
  • You possess advanced practical skills
  • Practice is necessary as your methodology
  • You can document rigorously
  • Your project will generate shareable insights
  • You have realistic scope and resources

⚠️ Common Misconceptions:

  • ❌ "All creative practice is research"
    ✅ Only investigative, innovative practice with shareable insights
  • ❌ "I'll figure out the research question as I make"
    ✅ You need a clear inquiry from the start (though it may evolve)
  • ❌ "PaR means less writing"
    ✅ You'll write 30,000-40,000 words PLUS conduct extensive practice
  • ❌ "My practice speaks for itself"
    ✅ You must "attend differently" and articulate the research dimension

Practice as Research vs. Practice-Led Research

Practice as Research (PaR)

The artistic practice is central to both the inquiry AND the output. Knowledge emerges through the process of making. Findings are expressed as artworks/performances alongside written reflection.

Common in: Performance studies, visual art, music

Practice-Led Research

Focuses on investigating the process or context of creative practice. Involves critical analysis of HOW and WHY a practice is developed, rather than claiming practice itself constitutes knowledge.

Common in: Design, architecture, creative industries

Writing & Editing

Like sleep, writing is a habit that needs four pillars: Duration, Depth, Continuity, and Regularity

Essential Writing Tips

🎯

Don't Mix Modes

Note-taking, Writing, and Editing are different modes. Don't mix them!

  • Do hard thinking when note-taking
  • Write quickly (get ducks in a row)
  • Edit slowly
💭

Writing as Thinking

Do you REALLY know something if you can't write it down? Do the hard work of writing your notes in your own words.

🔖

Use Bookmarks

When you read a book, you don't start at the beginning to find where you left off. Create a bookmark in your writing:

"Completed section on music in 1920s → Next: write linking section on Walter Benjamin for RQ2"
🎯

Research with Intention

Before hitting Google Scholar, articulate your intention:

"Today I'm looking at [A], to find [B], because that will allow me to [C]. This is important because [D]."
📝

Form a Habit

Depth & Duration: Schedule and protect your writing time

Continuity & Regularity: Can be done in smaller chunks, opportunistically

💬

Quotes as Building Blocks

Use quotes to build your argument:

  • This interests me because X
  • This differs from my approach because Y
  • This conflicts with Z

Editing is Continuity

In film, there's a continuity editor. As a viewer, you'll be 'jolted out' if you notice a hairstyle change or window going from open to shut between cuts. The same goes for your thesis. Editing ensures consistency throughout.
